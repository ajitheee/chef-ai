# Universal Culinary Production Knowledge Pack

## Global recipe scaling production mathematics and validation reference

| Version | 3.0 |
|---|---|
| Effective date | September 2026 |
| Operational owner | Chef Kris Arguin |
| Status | Current governing document |

# Purpose and rule classes

•  This pack contains universal production mathematics, culinary scaling methods, validation logic, and configurable working assumptions.
•  Universal formula means mathematically stable across operations.
•  Working assumption means useful only until a recipe test, product specification, organization rule, or production record replaces it.
•  Verified value means supported by an approved recipe, product specification, label, test, or production record.
•  Facility rule and jurisdiction rule belong in overlays, not in the universal pack.

# Core finished yield equations

•  Required finished yield by weight = approved portions × finished weight per portion.
•  Required finished yield by volume = approved portions × finished volume per portion.
•  Scale factor = target finished yield ÷ verified baseline finished yield.
•  Scaled stable ingredient = baseline ingredient × scale factor.
•  Actual portions = actual finished yield ÷ approved finished portion.
•  Overage or shortage = actual finished yield − required finished yield.
•  Portion variance percentage = (actual portion − standard portion) ÷ standard portion × 100.

# AP EP and process yield

•  Yield percentage = edible or finished quantity ÷ as-purchased or raw quantity.
•  Required AP quantity = required EP or finished quantity ÷ yield percentage.
•  Expected EP or finished quantity = AP or raw quantity × yield percentage.
•  Trim loss = AP − EP.
•  Cook loss = raw edible quantity − cooked finished quantity.
•  Drain yield, thaw loss, bone loss, skin loss, purge, evaporation, absorption, and transfer loss must be modeled separately when material.
•  Never apply one generic yield percentage across different products, cuts, suppliers, or cooking methods without labeling it an assumption.

# Weight volume and density

•  Weight and volume are not interchangeable without density or a verified measured conversion.
•  Use weight for meats, produce, dry goods, and finished components when portion control depends on mass.
•  Use volume for broths, beverages, soups, sauces, dressings, mayos, salsas, and liquid condiments when service and purchasing use volume.
•  Use count for pieces, buns, tortillas, pizzas, pans, skewers, packages, or other discrete units.
•  For thick or particulate foods, verify that the service utensil delivers both solids and liquid in the intended ratio.
•  When converting a volume-based source to weight, measure the actual product density instead of using a generic ingredient density.

# Unit normalization and precision

•  Normalize all source quantities into one internal unit system before calculating.
•  Retain full calculation precision internally and round once at the final operational step.
•  1 lb = 16 oz; 1 kg = 1,000 g; 1 US gal = 128 US fl oz; 1 US qt = 32 US fl oz; 1 US cup = 8 US fl oz.
•  US fluid ounces are volume and ounces are weight; never treat them as equivalent.
•  Use verified local standards for cups, tablespoons, teaspoons, gallons, and pints because definitions vary by region.
•  Display kitchen-friendly fractions only after the unrounded total is validated.

# Rounding hierarchy

•  Do not round early.
•  Round pieces, packages, pans, and cuts to whole executable units.
•  Round purchasing quantities upward only after subtracting usable inventory when inventory use is authorized.
•  Bulk low-impact ingredients may be rounded for production practicality if the finished yield remains intact.
•  High-impact ingredients require conservative rounding, test batches, or staged additions.
•  Show both target yield and actual rounded yield when operational rounding changes the count.

# High impact ingredients

•  Review salt, curing agents, chiles, pepper extracts, fish sauce, soy sauce, vinegar, citrus, alcohol, smoke flavor, flavoring oils, sesame oil, extracts, concentrates, bouillon, bases, leaveners, stabilizers, gums, and enzymes independently.
•  Linear scaling is a starting calculation, not automatic authorization.
•  Account for concentration, surface area, evaporation, absorption, holding time, carryover seasoning, and repeated sauce applications.
•  Use staged additions with a documented maximum when sensory testing is required.
•  Never round a trace extract, cure, chemical leavener, or concentrated additive to a convenient large unit.

# Sub recipe dependency scaling

•  Calculate the parent recipe’s required finished amount of each sub-recipe.
•  Divide required sub-recipe output by its verified baseline finished yield to obtain the sub-recipe scale factor.
•  Round the sub-recipe batch only after identifying equipment minimums, package units, and acceptable overage.
•  Feed the actual rounded sub-recipe output back into the parent validation.
•  Consolidate common sub-recipes across menu items only when the formulation and allergen profile are identical.
•  Prevent the same sub-recipe ingredients from being counted both within the parent and again as a separate requirement.

# Build reconciliation

•  For composed dishes, sum the per-portion components and confirm they equal the stated finished portion.
•  For bulk mixed dishes, sum expected finished component weights and compare with the stated batch yield.
•  Document process loss, evaporation, retained cooking liquid, draining, or trim when the totals do not reconcile directly.
•  For sauces absorbed during holding, distinguish amount produced, amount added, amount retained, and amount served.
•  For split service, allocate the master batch by location, station, or meal period and prevent duplicate procurement.

# Proteins

•  Scale cooked portions from cooked finished yield; convert to raw purchasing quantity using a verified yield for the exact cut and method.
•  Bone-in, skin-on, trimmed, marinated, injected, breaded, frozen, and precooked products require different yield logic.
•  For count-based proteins such as shrimp, wings, ribs, or fillets, reconcile average piece weight with portion count.
•  Account for purge, thaw loss, trim, cooking loss, carryover, slicing loss, and holding loss when material.
•  Do not use safe minimum temperature as the tenderness endpoint for a braised cut.

# Rice grains legumes and absorbed liquids

•  Do not confuse dry weight with cooked yield.
•  Use the verified cooked-yield ratio for the exact product and method. Generic ratios are working assumptions only.
•  Required dry amount = required cooked amount ÷ verified cooked-yield multiplier.
•  Separate dry grain, cooking liquid, cooked target, drained target when applicable, and final incorporated amount.
•  Forecast by finished weight rather than visual pan fullness.
•  Verify the actual service scoop by weighing multiple normal portions.
•  For legumes, distinguish dry, soaked, cooked, drained, and liquid-retained weights.

# Pasta noodles and hydration

•  Track dry, fresh, frozen, precooked, soaked, cooked, drained, and finished weights distinctly.
•  Use an actual yield for the product and doneness standard; generic doubling is only a working assumption.
•  Account for sauce absorption during holding and avoid treating absorbed sauce as unexplained yield.
•  Validate that noodle or pasta quantity is meaningful for the menu identity.
•  Plan shallow holding, staged saucing, or small batches when continued absorption damages quality.

# Vegetables fruits and herbs

•  Apply trim and cooking yield to the exact form purchased.
•  Distinguish whole, peeled, seeded, cored, trimmed, drained, thawed, cooked, and finished states.
•  Leafy herbs and garnishes often require count, bunch, case, or usable-weight conversion verified from the supplier.
•  Do not reduce a recipe because of an ordering or delivery overage unless production records show actual recipe overuse.
•  Account for water loss, compression, oxidation, and holding when they materially affect portion or quality.

# Sauces dressings soups and beverages

•  Scale by finished volume or verified finished weight, not by the sum of unprocessed ingredient volumes.
•  Required volume = portions × volume per portion plus only an approved production allowance.
•  Account for evaporation, straining, blending expansion, retained solids, absorption, kettle heel, transfer loss, and service residue.
•  For chunky soups and sauces, verify the intended solids-to-liquid ratio and utensil delivery.
•  A slurry, roux, reduction, puree, starch, gum, or emulsion must be based on the tested final texture rather than added automatically.
•  For beverages and concentrates, use the manufacturer dilution ratio unless an approved tested formulation replaces it.

# Baking dough and portioned products

•  Baker’s percentage expresses each ingredient as a percentage of flour weight; total dough weight must still reconcile to piece count.
•  Dough required = pieces or pans × dough weight per piece or pan.
•  Account for divider loss, bowl residue, proofing limits, bake loss, icing, filling, and finishing yield.
•  Pan size, piece weight, proofing method, bake profile, and cut count are required for reproducible scaling.
•  Frozen preformed or pre-sheeted products follow verified piece count and package specifications rather than scratch dough assumptions.

# Pans vessels packages and equipment capacity

•  Required containers = required finished quantity ÷ approved usable quantity per container, rounded to a whole container.
•  Never use nominal vessel capacity as safe working capacity.
•  Respect maximum fill depth, headspace, circulation, rack loading, recovery time, mixing limits, and safe handling weight.
•  Divide batches so each batch uses the same formulation ratio and receives consistent cook, cool, and hold treatment.
•  Package ordering and recipe use are separate: purchasing may round to a case while production uses only the approved amount.

# Cut count and discrete yield

•  Required whole items = target portions ÷ portions per whole item, rounded up.
•  Actual portions = rounded whole items × portions per whole item.
•  Use this logic for pizzas, cakes, sandwiches cut into portions, sheet pans, loaves, packages, skewers, and similar items.
•  State the whole-item count, cut count, target count, and actual count.

# Batch sequencing holding and replenishment

•  A scaled recipe must specify whether the full quantity is produced at once or in controlled batches.
•  Model cook time, recovery time, cooling capacity, holding duration, transport, service rate, and replenishment frequency.
•  Crispy foods generally require ventilation and late finishing; starches and noodles may absorb liquid; emulsions may break; vegetables may continue cooking.
•  Quality limits may require smaller batches even when equipment can hold the full volume.
•  Food-safety limits must come from the configured jurisdiction and approved organizational policy.

# Procurement consolidation

•  Convert each recipe requirement to a common purchasing unit only after recipe validation.
•  Consolidate identical products across recipes; do not consolidate products with different specifications or allergen profiles.
•  Separate exact usable demand, authorized inventory deduction, purchase requirement, case pack, cases ordered, and expected remainder.
•  Do not invent case packs or supplier yields.
•  Keep fresh, frozen, refrigerated, dry, chemical, specialty, and venue-specific order streams separate when the organization requires it.

# Forecasting with production records

•  Forecast from comparable events, venue traffic, menu role, station take rate, weather, calendar, price, audience, and service duration when data exists.
•  Record planned portions, actual production, portions served, leftovers, waste, transfers, sell-outs, and substitutions.
•  Adjusted forecast is a decision, not a recipe change.
•  Update a recipe yield only when the formulation or verified production yield changed.
•  Preserve event context so a one-time anomaly is not generalized worldwide.

# Validation tests before release

•  Count × portion equals target finished yield.
•  Component build equals finished portion.
•  Baseline yield is finished yield, not ingredient total.
•  Raw purchasing quantity supports the cooked requirement.
•  All sub-recipes are present and scaled once.
•  High-impact ingredients received independent review.
•  Batch and vessel capacities are executable.
•  Target and actual rounded yield are visible.
•  Recipe, prep, and procurement totals agree.
•  Units, locale, regulatory status, allergens, and verification flags are complete.
